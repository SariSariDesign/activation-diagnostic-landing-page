Friction-Heavy, but Fixable

Measured against best-practice activation patterns for health and wellness products, your flow carries friction at several points where users decide whether to continue. Products in this range usually see meaningful signup-to-activation drop-off, and it is the recoverable kind. The top findings in this scorecard are the places to start.

Findings

Top 5 Findings

The 5 highest-impact friction points, in the order a user hits them, grouped by page across the flow. Selected from a 13-finding evaluation across 11 UX dimensions. Select "See more details" on any finding for the evidence and fix.

Homepage

1
Performance & Core Web Vitals Major

Real visitors on phones wait 3.7 seconds for Nervana’s main content

Google’s real-user data for trynervana.com on mobile fails Core Web Vitals: the largest element takes 3.7 seconds to appear, and so does the first content. In Google’s lab test on a mid-range phone the homepage scores 53 out of 100, with main content at 9.9 seconds and 2.1 seconds spent running JavaScript. Desktop scores 83, so the problem is mobile-specific.

Why This Finding Matters

People looking for help with chronic symptoms mostly arrive on their phones, and the hero and quiz button are the first thing that has to load. Every second of blank or half-loaded page is a chance to bounce back to search results before the quiz, Nervana’s main activation hook, is even visible.
PERF-01 PERF-02 PERF-06 PERF-09
Recommendation
  • Serve the hero photo in WebP or AVIF, sized for phone screens, and preload it. Google estimates about 186 KiB of image savings.
  • Defer the scripts and styles that block the first paint (about 970 ms of estimated savings) and remove unused JavaScript (about 298 KiB).
  • Switch the 10 animations Google flags to transform and opacity, or start them after the page has loaded.
Effort: Medium Impact: High

Symptom quiz

2
Interaction Design Minor

“Take the symptom quiz” lands almost two phone screens above the first question

On a phone-width screen, the quiz page opens on a title, author line, update date, medical note, and a three-step explainer. The first question starts about 1,100px down and the first answer button sits around 1,515px, so a visitor scrolls nearly two full screens before they can answer. On desktop the question card also starts below the fold.

Why This Finding Matters

The homepage promises “Start with a short quiz,” and people who tap that button have already said yes. Opening on an article header instead of question 1 adds a pause at the exact moment their momentum is highest.
IXD-05 ONB-03 MOB-04
Recommendation
  • Point the homepage quiz button at the quiz card itself (for example, an anchor to #quiz) so it opens on question 1.
  • On mobile, move the author line and three-step explainer below the quiz, and keep the one-line medical note right next to the first question.
Effort: Low Impact: Medium
3
Onboarding & Activation Major

After 9 personal questions, results are locked behind an email and a newsletter sign-up

The quiz ends on “See your full pattern summary,” but the summary only appears after entering an email, which also subscribes the person to Nora’s newsletter. Directly under the email field, the card still says “Your answers stay in this browser and are not saved or sent to Nervana.” There is no way to see results without giving an email.

Why This Finding Matters

The homepage says the quiz will show whether Nervana is right for you, and that answer is what moves someone from curious to starting a trial. Holding it back after questions about symptoms and childhood experiences asks for trust right when the person has shared the most, and the privacy line beside the email box makes the ask feel inconsistent.
ONB-07 CRO-08 FORM-01
Recommendation
  • Show the pattern summary right after question 9, with the free-trial button directly under it.
  • Offer email as an optional next step after results (“Email me my summary”), with the newsletter as a separate, unchecked choice.
  • Reword the privacy line so it stays accurate at the email step, for example: “Your answers stay in this browser. If you add your email, we’ll use it to send your summary.”
Effort: Medium Impact: High

Free-trial signup

4
Conversion Rate Optimization Major

The free-trial page never says whether a card is needed, when billing starts, or how to cancel

“Try 7 days free” leads to a two-step page: create an account, then choose a plan. The 12-month plan is preselected and shown on its own, and monthly plans sit behind a “Need more flexibility?” toggle. Under the “Start 7-day free trial” button, the only line is “Have a promo code? Enter it on the next screen.”

Why This Finding Matters

For people who have already “tried everything,” a free trial that defaults to a yearly plan with no billing details reads as a commitment, not a trial. The questions this page leaves open are the ones that stop a click on “Start 7-day free trial,” which is Nervana’s core conversion.
CRO-10 CRO-08 COPY-09
Recommendation
  • Add one line under the button that states whether a card is required, the date of the first charge, and how to cancel before then.
  • Show monthly and annual plans side by side, with annual marked as the best value, instead of hiding monthly behind a toggle.
  • Tell people what the next screen is, for example: “Next: secure checkout, then download the app.”
Effort: Low Impact: High
5
Visual & Brand Design Major

Creating an account signs people up for the newsletter and says nothing about how their health information is handled

For a signed-out visitor, step 1 of the trial page is “Create an account” with a single note: “By creating an account, you’ll also receive Finding Nervana, Nora’s newsletter. Unsubscribe anytime.” The step carries no privacy or data-handling reassurance, even though members go on to discuss their “unique life situation” with an AI coach.

Why This Finding Matters

Health and wellness buyers expect privacy reassurance at the moment they hand over personal details, and an AI coach raises the stakes. Right now the only message at sign-up is a marketing opt-in, which is the opposite of what a cautious new member needs to see before creating an account.
VD-08 CRO-06
Recommendation
  • Add a short privacy note beside “Create account” that says what Nervana stores, who can see coach conversations, and links to the privacy policy.
  • Make the newsletter a separate, unchecked checkbox instead of a condition of creating an account.
Effort: Low Impact: Medium
What The Research Says

Patterns that bear on this flow

Documented research and case studies matched to the weakest areas of this flow: what moved the numbers for comparable products.

HealthCare.gov account setup: registration before value

10 usability guidelines broken

Nielsen Norman Group’s teardown of HealthCare.gov found that forcing account creation before people could see plans broke the give-before-you-ask exchange, and the bad first step dragged down trust in the whole service. Nervana’s quiz asks for an email before showing the results people just answered nine personal questions to get, which is the same pattern at a smaller scale.

Source: Nielsen Norman Group

August AI: privacy reassurance before sensitive data

42% abandonment

An AI health companion saw 42% of users abandon when sensitive data was requested too early, and responded by moving privacy badges and progress transparency to the front of onboarding. Nervana’s account step, the doorway to an AI coach, currently leads with a newsletter opt-in and no privacy note.

Source: Krsnarka portfolio case study (August AI)

Mobile load time and abandonment

53% of mobile visits abandoned after 3 seconds

Google’s mobile research found that more than half of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than 3 seconds to load. Nervana’s real-user mobile data puts main content at 3.7 seconds, just past that line, on the page that holds the quiz button.

Source: Google, “The need for mobile speed”

Methodology

An AI-assisted analyst evaluated 11 UX dimensions against a Nielsen Norman Group-grounded framework, with automated accessibility, performance, and SEO checks; this preview is 100% automated, with no designer walkthrough.

Evaluation Lens

Nervana sells a subscription app to people with chronic symptoms and relies on its website to turn quiz-takers and trial-clickers into members, so we weighted the quiz, sign-up, and trial steps most heavily and judged trust against health and wellness conventions.
